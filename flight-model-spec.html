01

Star Citizen: IFCS mechanics

The single most important framing, in the words of the engineer who wrote it: IFCS is not the flight model. The flight model is a plain 6‑DOF rigid‑body Newtonian simulation. IFCS is a control system layered on top that converts pilot intent into per‑thruster commands. Every characteristic behaviour below is a consequence of that split.

Ship motion is induced by its thrusters, with each individual thruster generating a limited amount of force at its mount point. Depending on the direction of a thruster and its mount point relative to the ship’s center of mass, each thruster will generate both a force and torque on the ship. John Pritchett, CIG Senior Physics Programmer — IFCS and Flight Physics, Star Citizen 3.2

1.1 The control pipeline

Six stages. A mod that reproduces the feel needs stages 2, 3 and 6 at minimum; stages 4 and 5 are what make it survive damage and disturbance.

1.2 Input is interpreted three different ways

This table is the heart of the coupled/decoupled distinction, and almost every guide gets it wrong. The mode does not change the physics; it changes what quantity the stick commands.

The decoupled definition, precisely

Decoupled is not “Newtonian mode” — the sim is always Newtonian. Decoupled changes exactly three things: throttle is disabled entirely; the three linear strafe axes switch from velocity command to acceleration command; and the translational-stability (anti-slide) subsystem stops trying to pin the velocity vector to the nose. Rotational control is byte-for-byte identical in both modes — still velocity-controlled, still damped, still limited. This is why decoupled in SC does not feel like Elite’s FA-off, which does remove rotational damping.

1.3 Thruster model and asymmetry

Four thruster classes, each a point force applied at a mount point, therefore each producing torque as a side effect:

  • Main — the majority of sustainable thrust and almost all forward thrust.
  • Retro — the majority of braking/backward thrust.
  • Maneuvering — scattered over the hull; provides some translation and the majority of attitude control, in intermittent bursts.
  • VTOL — sustainable vertical thrust for hover and vertical launch.
  • CMG (Control Moment Gyro) — a pure-torque generator whose mount position is irrelevant; usually near the cockpit so a ship retains minimal rotational authority after losing thrusters.

The PCS clusters available generators into pure force and pure torque groups: a pure force needs either one thruster firing through the COM or two-plus balanced so torques cancel; a pure torque needs two-plus whose forces cancel. Unbalanced thrusters are still allowed for linear control, with CMGs countering the parasitic torque and a clamp on how far attitude may drift — otherwise a damaged ship would lose all linear control.

1.4 Master Modes: SCM and NAV

Since Alpha 3.23 a ship is always in exactly one of two master modes. This replaced the older Precision / SCM / Cruise triad and the separate afterburner regime.

SCM — Standard Control Mode
  • Shields regenerate; weapons, countermeasures and all operator modes (GUNS, MISL, SCN, mining, salvage) live
  • Hard per-hull speed cap, typically 110–280 m/s
  • Full maneuverability; quantum drive unavailable
  • Boost may push slightly past the cap while capacitor lasts
NAV — Navigation Mode
  • Shields collapse into a buffer; weapons and countermeasures offline
  • Top speed ~870–1575 m/s, i.e. roughly 5× SCM
  • Quantum drive auto-spools; signature balloons
  • Operator modes: QTM (default), FLT (max engine thrust), SCN

Swap is a long press of B with a per-ship spool delay gated by quantum drive size. The tap-to-cycle variant ships deliberately unbound to prevent accidental swaps. Historically: Precision was the takeoff mode with heavily reduced max velocity and rescaled throttle; SCM derived its cap from F/m × T — the braking ability on the strongest turning axis; Cruise traded control for ~5× velocity with the nose locked to the velocity vector.

1.6 Boost

Boost is deliberately non-specific: it raises every thruster’s output, so the same button buys faster acceleration, harder braking, a tighter controlled turn radius, a higher terminal velocity in drag, or faster spin recovery. Since 3.23 there is one action — internally still v_afterburner, labelled “Boost”, on LShift.

The differentiator is ramp-up time, which scales with hull size (0.4 s on a light fighter, 2.0 s on a Hammerhead) — boost is not instant, and that ramp is a large part of why heavy ships feel heavy. The rotation multiplier is a flat ×1.20 on nearly every hull. Critically, boost multiplies rotation rates as well as thrust — it is an agility button, not just a speed button.

1.7 The assist subsystems

The feedforward controller sums contributions from independent subsystems. In 4.x each became individually bindable with a toggle/on/off triple — a good template for a mod’s control surface.

IFCS feedforward subsystems
SubsystemWhat it computes
Translational stability
anti-slide		Forward thrust to replace velocity lost in a turn, plus lateral/vertical thrust to cancel the velocity gained. With enough thrust a ship flies a perfect circle with zero slide at the expected nose-forward speed. Once velocity × turn rate exceeds available stabilising thrust, it starts sliding — and that threshold is the single most important feel parameter in the whole system.
Rotational stability

Because local axes are generally not the principal axes of inertia, sustained angular velocity generates large torques about the other axes; without correction the ship tumbles. 1.8 Rotation: the motion profile, and why it feels weighty

Rotational input is a velocity command in both coupled and decoupled. What gives it mass is the motion profile between the current and goal rate. SC explicitly rejects 2nd-order (instant acceleration change) control:

If we would allow our ships to switch between zero and maximum acceleration instantaneously, the motion of our ships would be extremely stiff and unnatural. Therefore, we place a limit on how quickly acceleration can change, and this limit is called “jerk”.Pritchett, IFCS 3.2
  • 2nd order (quadratic) — infinite jerk. Used only for capitals where accelerations are low.
  • 3rd order (cubic) — finite jerk, linear acceleration. Predictable; good for velocity control and waypoints, but too expensive when the control state changes every frame.
  • Transient (exponential, continuous in all higher-order derivatives) — the primary profile for fighters and racers. Restored as the default in IFCS 3.0 after a 3rd-order interlude.

Feedback is a critically damped PID in the Reaction Control System, correcting the error between the feedforward profile and measured state. Design consequence: there is no overshoot-and-oscillate; there is a settling time, and the ship never snaps.

The system also injects deliberate imperfection — a low-level thruster error overlay on all flight, scaling with damage, overheating and component failure. It shows up as subtle nose play when firing at capacity or settling at zero velocity, and becomes extreme under serious damage.

1.9 ESP — Enhanced Stick Precision

ESP is far more sophisticated than the “sensitivity dampening near the target” description in circulation. It is a blend toward an ideal tracking solution, gated by how well the player’s own input already correlates with that solution. The design goal is that the player cannot detect it until it is switched off.

  1. Query the targeting system for time-to-hit t with the last-fired weapon.
  2. Project target position: TargetPos = EnemyPos + ΔVel·t + 0.5·ΔAccel·t²
  3. Compute match rates — the pitch/yaw rates that hold the target at a constant screen position without closing: Radial = TargetPos − ShipPos; RadialVel = (TargetVel·Radial / Radial·Radial)·Radial; TanVel = TargetVel − RadialVel; ω = |TanVel| / |Radial|.
  4. Compute close rates — the rates that bring the nose onto the radial vector with zero overshoot.
  5. Blend match and close by angular correlation between the ship’s forward vector and the radial vector, using a tight exponential: full correlation at 0°, zero correlation at ~2.4°. At full correlation the goal is match only (track without being dragged onto centre-of-mass) — this small window is what lets the player refine the shot relative to the ideal solution, and is explicitly called out as an essential part of the design.
  6. Blend the player’s raw pitch/yaw with the goal rates by the correlation between them, per axis, by magnitude and direction. Higher correlation → more blend.
  7. Return the corrected rates to IFCS in place of the raw input.

ESP ships enabled with no default key. CIG’s own recommendation for using it well: turn off comstab and G-safe.

1.10 Atmospheric flight

SC ships are always thruster-flown; they do not rely on aerodynamic lift even in atmosphere. The atmospheric layer adds: velocity-squared drag with a per-axis coefficient and a dynamically computed cross-sectional area per local axis; a centre of pressure (the area centroid) that generates torque whenever it is offset from the COM along the velocity vector; placeholder turbulence scaled by density and airspeed; an altitude-dependent density model; a terminal velocity that clamps below the artificial cap at low altitude or with degraded thrusters; and a safe velocity above which drag loads damage the structure. Lift is explicitly not implemented. Real control surfaces and stall are still forward-looking work.

Three corrections to stale guides

Decouple is C, not CapsLock — CapsLock appears in no shipped profile from 2.x onward; it was V pre-3.23, then moved to C, displacing cruise control to LAlt+C. The bare mouse wheel is no longer the speed limiter — in 4.x it cycles weapon groups. And v_target_match_vel (“Match Target Speed”) was removed between 3.23.1 and 4.8.1; SC currently has no match-target-velocity feature.

2.2 VJoy vs Relative: the integrator

VJoy mode — the default
  • Mouse deltas are integrated into the deflection of a virtual stick — a persistent state variable, drawn on the HUD as a line from screen centre to the reticle
  • Deflection maps to a commanded angular rate
  • The virtual stick does not self-centre. Stop moving the mouse and the ship keeps rotating at that rate indefinitely
  • Because the reticle has a real screen position, unlocked gimbals can slave to it
  • ESP operates on this reticle
Relative mode — “FPS style”
  • Per-frame mouse delta is the rate command — no cursor, no persistent offset
  • Stop moving the mouse and the command returns to zero; rotation stops
  • Effectively a spring-centred stick with infinite spring rate
  • With no off-centre reticle, gimbals are effectively locked forward — the ship is the aiming device
  • Per-axis sensitivity and inversion under av_relative_mode

The mechanical statement: every SC input path terminates in a goal rate, never a goal attitude — there is no position-control loop from player to ship orientation anywhere in the stack. VJoy inserts an extra integrator between hand and rate command, so the mouse effectively controls rotational acceleration, two integrations from attitude. Relative mode removes it: one integration, exactly like an FPS camera. That second-order versus first-order plant is the entire “floaty vs direct” argument — and it is why relative-mode players stop on target more cleanly while VJoy players hold steadier low rates. Underneath both sits the jerk limit, a third-order constraint added in 3.10.

The historical Interactive Mode was the free-floating-crosshair scheme whose competitive advantage was that a mouse user could hold a firing solution the airframe itself could never achieve. CIG did not delete it — in 3.10 they made VJoy the default and separated the aim-point channel from the flight-command channel, gating gimbal pointing behind an explicit gimbal state and an explicit source selection (VJoy vs View). That separation, not the removal of an input mode, is what neutralised the advantage.

Input conditioning: mouse acceleration and mouse smoothing both default to 0.00. There is no mouse deadzone setting — the “deadzone” players describe is the VJoy integrator’s near-centre behaviour. Sensitivity curves are piecewise-linear <nonlinearity_curve> lookups per axis per device, arranged in an inheriting hierarchy; the shipped gamepad curve attenuates ~85% over the first 30% of travel.

03

Elite Dangerous, for contrast

Elite is also a Newtonian rigid body, but wrapped in a much harder fly‑by‑wire layer. Frontier are explicit that the restrictions are arbitrary and deliberate: “a pretty complex set of arbitrary restrictions overlaid on a Newtonian mechanics model, designed to work together to create the atmospheric flight of cinematic sci-fi.”

3.1 The four hard clamps

  • Maximum speed is limited by ship computers — a drag-like clamp, not a thrust limit. Frontier’s stated reason is purely combat framing: faster ships end up further apart, which ruins shot quality and the ability to read an opponent.
  • Rotation rates are arbitrarily throttled to a fixed per-hull ωmax.
  • Thruster power is reduced within 25 m/s of both 0 and max speed — a deliberate soft zone at each end, mostly a docking aid.
  • Maneuvering thrusters offer significantly greater thrust than the main engine.

Universal direction caps, constant across every hull: reverse = 60% of forward max; lateral/vertical = 80% of forward max. Measured acceleration asymmetry runs lateral ≈ 0.66× forward (median over 26 hulls; range 0.29 on an Imperial Cutter to 1.00 on an Anaconda) and reverse ≈ 0.78× forward.

3.2 Flight Assist

FA is three separable behaviours, and understanding them separately is what makes the SC comparison legible:

  1. Linear velocity servo. Throttle is a speed request: FA drives |v| toward throttle × v_max using main and retro thrust. This is why boost decays fast with FA on — the ship actively retro-thrusts back to the setpoint.
  2. Drift nulling. Maneuvering thrusters drive the off-axis velocity components to zero, pinning the velocity vector to the nose vector.
  3. Rotational damping. Release the stick and counter-torque drives ω to zero.

FA-off removes all three. The caps remain — the flight computer still prevents unsafe speeds and spin. The rate uplift from FA-off is widely overstated: ωmax is fixed and FA-off does not raise it. What FA-off does is bypass the speed-dependent rate penalty and free the thruster budget from drift-cancelling duty. So:

Rotational Correction is a separate sub-mode, not the same as rotational damping: it exists to match a rotating starport’s spin. Disable FA while it is on and the ship keeps spinning with the station. FA decomposes into five independently disableable parts: Auto Dock, Auto Launch, Auto Land, Rotational Correction, Hyperspace Dethrottle.

3.3 The blue zone

A smooth speed-dependent multiplier on angular rate, peaking at exactly 50% of current max speed. Frontier designer Mike Evans, on record: “The exact centre is where you get the best turn rates. Any deviation away from that will result in less than optimal turn rates.” Two properties a mod must get right:

  • It is a curve, not a step, and its steepness is per-hull — an FAS stays agile at the band edge, a Fer-de-Lance falls off hard.
  • Because v_max scales with ENG pips, the blue zone slides along the throttle tape with pip allocation. It is 50% of the pip-adjusted max, not 50% of the tape.
  • The tape reads speed magnitude regardless of direction — drifting sideways at 50% of max also gives peak turn rates. This is precisely what makes reverski work.

3.4 Yaw, deliberately crippled

Fleet-wide yaw ÷ pitch: median 0.40; yaw ÷ roll: ~0.16. Worst offenders sit at 0.25 (Cobra Mk III) and the Cutter/Corvette/Type-9 yaw at 8 °/s — a 45-second standing 360. The intent is stated outright in Frontier’s own FAQ: without it you get “endless turreting and circle-strafing… due to the lack of terrain features in mid space”, so they limit yaw and enforce an optimum corner speed, “forcing [you] to roll and then pitch to get the most efficient turn rate.” Compare SC’s 0.76–0.91. Note that post-2024 Elite hulls walk this back hard — Mandalay 0.80, Cobra Mk V 0.73.

3.5 Boost and pips

  • Multiplier is per-hull, 1.22–1.70× (median 1.38), inversely correlated with base speed — fast ships get small multipliers.
  • Cost is a fixed MJ draw from the ENG capacitor, 7–27 MJ by hull, not a percentage. If the fitted distributor’s capacity is below the boost cost you cannot boost at all, even at full charge.
  • Cooldown is emergent, not a timer: t = boostEnergy ÷ (engrate × pips/4) — roughly 4.8–11.7 s at 4 pips, doubling at 2 pips, never at 0 pips.
  • Boost grants peak turn rate for its whole duration, which is why the boost-flip works despite putting you far above blue-zone speed.
  • v_max is linear in ENG pips but the slope is a hidden per-hull constant: a Vulture keeps 90.5% of top speed at zero pips, a Type-9 keeps 30.8%.

3.6 FA-off drift combat

The canonical maneuver is the boost-flip: boost → full pitch → FA-off → hold until the nose crosses → FA-on. Boost pushes |v| far above cap where FA-on would crush pitch rate by ×2.2–3.2; FA-off removes that coupling so you rotate at peak ω while the velocity vector keeps its heading. Reverski exploits the tape reading magnitude: reverse at 60% of the reverse cap and you are still in the blue zone. Drift turning works because FA-on spends thruster budget fighting the drift its own turn creates — FA-off gives that budget back to rotation and lets the lateral impulse translate you into the turn.

The important divergence

Elite’s FA-off is a momentary tactical mode used for the rotation phase of a maneuver and then dropped — experienced players are openly split on whether it is even a net gain in PvE. Star Citizen’s decoupled is likewise recommended as a brief orientation change, for a different reason: sitting on a constant vector gets you killed. Neither game rewards flying permanently decoupled. A mod that only implements “a decouple toggle” and stops has implemented the least-used 5% of either flight model.

04

What actually creates the weight

Against the baseline of a ship that always flies where its nose points, seven concrete properties do essentially all the work. They are listed in descending order of contribution to the characteristic feel.

1. Orientation and velocity are separate state

The arcade model stores a speed scalar and applies it along the forward vector; turning the nose instantly redirects the entire velocity. The simulation model stores a velocity vector in world space that only changes when force is applied. Every other property on this list is downstream of that one representation change. It is also the single hardest thing to retrofit, because it changes the shape of the state, not just its values.

2. Momentum conservation with finite, asymmetric thrust

Weight comes from the ratio of available thrust to mass, not from thrust being low. Concretely: 0 → SCM takes 1.4–2.5 s on a light fighter and 3–5.4 s on a capital. Braking is separately and deliberately weaker — retro at 20–35% of main means stopping takes three to five times longer than starting. That asymmetry is felt constantly and is cheap to implement.

3. Angular inertia with a jerk-limited rate profile

Rotation is a commanded rate, but the transition between rates is bounded in the third derivative. No snap, no oscillation, a real settling time. Combined with a moment of inertia that differs per axis, this produces the characteristic roll ≫ pitch > yaw hierarchy without any special-casing — and produces it differently for every hull as a consequence of geometry.

4. Thruster asymmetry as a geometric fact

Because thrust is applied at mount points, per-axis capability is not a tuning table — it emerges. Forward > vertical > lateral > reverse is the shape both games converge on. Elite states it as universal constants (80% lateral, 60% reverse); SC lets it fall out of thruster placement. Either is fine; having no asymmetry is what reads as arcade.

5. The slide threshold

The most underrated property. An assist that perfectly cancels drift feels like an arcade game; an assist that tries and fails past a threshold feels like a spacecraft. In SC, translational stability holds a perfect zero-slide circle until velocity × turn rate exceeds available lateral thrust — then the ship starts sliding, progressively, and the pilot feels the airframe reach its limit. Elite reaches the same place from the other direction with the blue-zone rate penalty. Reproduce the threshold, not just the assist.

6. Speed–agility coupling

Both games make going fast cost you turning. Elite does it explicitly via the blue-zone curve (×1.2–×3.2 penalty at the extremes). SC does it implicitly — comstab actively slows you to a speed you can corner at, and the SCM cap is derived from braking ability on the strongest turning axis. This is what makes throttle management a skill rather than a binary.

7. Deliberate imperfection

Low-magnitude thruster error on all flight; a settling PID rather than an exact solve; turbulence in atmosphere; unbalanced thrusters causing small attitude drift under linear thrust. These read as machinery. Perfect control reads as a spreadsheet.

The negative result worth internalising

Chris Roberts on why decoupled “turreting” is not the overpowered strategy it appears: “Sitting still or moving in a constant vector — which is what happens when you decouple — will get you killed.” The weight does not come from letting the player drift; it comes from making every change of vector cost time and commitment. If your mod makes drifting free and precise, you will have built something that feels less weighty than the arcade model, not more.

06

Target-engine reality check

This project sits in a No Man’s Sky mod directory, so one thing is worth stating before the checklist gets costed: the Core tier is not reachable by editing GCSPACESHIPGLOBALS.GLOBAL.MBIN. Here is exactly why, from the shipped struct definitions.

NMS tunes flight through GcPlayerSpaceshipControlData, which holds four GcPlayerSpaceshipEngineData profiles — SpaceEngine, PlanetEngine, CombatEngine, AtmosCombatEngine — per ship class (Control, ControlLight, ControlHeavy, ControlHover, ControlHeavyHover, ControlCorvette). The engine struct’s complete field set:

GcPlayerSpaceshipEngineData — every flight field
GroupFieldsWhat it tells you
LinearMaxSpeed, MinSpeed, ThrustForce, MinSpeedForce, Falloff, OverspeedBrake, ReverseBrakeMinSpeed exists — the model has a floor speed, not a zero. Thrust is a scalar along one axis.
Nose-followDirectionBrake, DirectionBrakeMinThis is the “flies where the nose points” mechanism — velocity is braked toward the facing vector. It is a tunable, so drift can be increased, but it cannot be replaced with a velocity servo.
TurningTurnStrength, TurnBrakeMin, TurnBrakeMax, LowSpeedTurnDamperA speed–agility coupling already exists in spirit (turning brakes you).
Auto-bankRollAmount, RollForce, RollAutoTimePlus RudderToRoll* in globals — yaw induces roll automatically.
BoostBoostMaxSpeed, BoostThrustForce, BoostFalloff, BoostingTurnDampBoost already damps turning. No capacitor, no ramp, no rotation multiplier.
Follow PIDFollowProportionalGain, FollowIntegralGain, FollowDerivativeGain, …Limit, IntegralDecayA real PID exists — but for follow/autopilot, not pilot control.

Globals adds LinearDamping, AngularDamping, DriftSpring, DriftTurnStrengthMultiplier, DriftTurnBrakeMultiplier, LateralDriftRange, ThrustDecaySpring, KBThrustSmoothTime, MaxOverspeedBrake, ShipThrustReverseThreshhold, plus StickyTurn* and AimSpeedTrack* (an existing aim-assist analogue).

What is missing, and it is the whole game

  • No lateral or vertical thrust axis exists anywhere in the struct. No strafe fields, no per-axis thrust, no 6DOF. Core items 3, 4, 6 and 7 have nothing to bind to.
  • No decouple concept. DirectionBrake can be lowered toward zero but the model still has a single thrust axis, so “decoupled” would mean drifting with no ability to translate.
  • No mass, no moment of inertia, no torque — MaxTorque and AngularFactor are single scalars in control data, not a tensor.
  • MinSpeed means the ship cannot come to rest.

The honest costing

Data-only mod (MBIN edits) reaches, at best, a partial Important tier: heavier accel/brake asymmetry via ThrustForce / ReverseBrake / OverspeedBrake; substantially more drift by cutting DirectionBrake and LinearDamping and raising DriftSpring; weightier rotation via AngularDamping, TurnStrength and KBThrustSmoothTime; a boost that costs agility via BoostingTurnDamp; and a genuine two-master-mode split by re-tuning CombatEngine vs SpaceEngine as SCM vs NAV, which is the one place NMS’s existing structure lines up neatly with SC’s. That is a real, worthwhile mod — it just is not “IFCS with no compromises.”

“No compromises” requires a native code mod that intercepts the ship physics update and substitutes its own integrator, plus new input bindings for six translation axes. Decide which of these two projects you are doing before costing the checklist, because the answer changes which tier is even addressable.
